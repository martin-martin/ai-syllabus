# But what is a neural network? | Deep learning chapter 1

**Who:** Grant Sanderson, 3Blue1Brown (math explainer on YouTube; not a researcher)
**What / when:** video, 2017-10-05, 3Blue1Brown YouTube channel (chapter 1 of the "Neural networks" series)
**Full piece:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aircAruvnKk
**Jump in here:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aircAruvnKk&t=696s (11:36–13:26, ~2 min; optional follow-up 15:17–16:13, "it's just a function")

## Why this one

Probably the most-watched introduction to neural networks, and a reference point for how to explain them visually. In this section Sanderson counts up the parameters of his tiny digit-recognizing network (about 13,000) and suggests imagining setting them all by hand, which is a good teaching move and a nice then-vs-now contrast with today's billion-parameter models. Pure education angle, and a break from interviews and papers.

## What's said there

> "One thought experiment that is at once fun and kind of horrifying is to imagine sitting down and setting all of these weights and biases by hand, purposefully tweaking the numbers so that the second layer picks up on edges, the third layer picks up on patterns, etc." (12:42, transcript verified: yes)

- Each neuron in the hidden layer connects to all 784 pixel neurons, each connection with its own weight, plus one bias per neuron. "All said and done, this network has almost exactly 13,000 total weights and biases." (12:18)
- "Learning" then just means getting the computer to find a valid setting for all of those numbers so it solves the problem (12:31).
- He finds hand-reasoning about weights more satisfying than treating the network as a black box: when it misbehaves, understanding what the weights mean gives you "a starting place for experimenting" (12:56–13:13), and when it works for unexpected reasons, it helps "challenge your assumptions" (13:14–13:22).
- Follow-up at 15:39: "Really the entire network is just a function, one that takes in 784 numbers as an input and spits out 10 numbers as an output." He calls it "an absurdly complicated function" and finds it reassuring that it looks complicated.
- Coda with Lisha Li (17:03 onward): sigmoid is "kind of old school", ReLU turned out much easier to train for very deep networks.

**B, educator's angle with a question (medium):**

Grant Sanderson (the math explainer behind 3Blue1Brown) starts his 2017 intro to neural networks by defining a neuron as "a thing that holds a number". A few minutes later he asks you to imagine tuning all ~13,000 weights and biases of his digit-recognizing network by hand: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aircAruvnKk&t=696s

His reason for doing this instead of treating the network as a black box: when it misbehaves, having some sense of what the weights mean gives you "a starting place for experimenting".

Curious if anyone uses a hand-tuning exercise like this when teaching (or learning) neural nets, or whether it's more useful as a thought experiment.

**C, then vs. now (longer):**

In 2017, Grant Sanderson (3Blue1Brown) explained neural networks with a small network that recognizes handwritten digits: 784 input pixels, two hidden layers of 16 neurons, and 10 outputs. All together, "almost exactly 13,000 total weights and biases".

He suggests imagining setting all of them by hand, so that one layer picks up on edges and the next on patterns. He calls it a thought experiment that is "at once fun and kind of horrifying": https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aircAruvnKk&t=696s

A bit later he describes the whole thing as "an absurdly complicated function" with 13,000 parameters. Three years after the video, GPT-3 had 175 billion.

I wonder how much of today's interpretability research comes down to that jump, from knobs you could in principle turn yourself to numbers nobody will ever look at one by one. Either way, a good 2 minutes to understand what a parameter actually is.
