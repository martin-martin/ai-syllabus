# Dropout: A Simple Way to Prevent Neural Networks from Overfitting

**Who:** Nitish Srivastava, Geoffrey Hinton, Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, Ruslan Salakhutdinov, University of Toronto
**What / when:** paper, Journal of Machine Learning Research 15 (2014), pp. 1929–1958 (submitted 11/13, published 6/14)
**Full piece:** https://jmlr.org/papers/v15/srivastava14a.html
**Jump in here:** https://jmlr.org/papers/volume15/srivastava14a/srivastava14a.pdf#page=4 (section 2 "Motivation", two paragraphs on p. 4 of the PDF, ~2 min read)

## Why this one

Dropout (randomly switching off units during training) became one of the standard tricks of the deep learning boom, and the paper explains it with an unusually readable analogy section before any math. The motivation comes straight from evolutionary biology, a theory of why sexual reproduction wins over asexual reproduction, plus a second, funnier analogy about conspiracies. Good for an education-minded audience: it shows how researchers reason by analogy, and it fits Martin's biology background.

## What's said there

> "A motivation for dropout comes from a theory of the role of sex in evolution (Livnat et al., 2010)." (section 2, p. 4, verified in the JMLR PDF: yes)

> "Since a gene cannot rely on a large set of partners to be present at all times, it must learn to do something useful on its own or in collaboration with a small number of other genes." (section 2, p. 4, verified: yes)

> "Ten conspiracies each involving five people is probably a better way to create havoc than one big conspiracy that requires fifty people to all play their parts correctly." (section 2, p. 4, verified: yes)

- Puzzle they start from: asexual reproduction should seem better for fitness, since a set of genes that works well together gets passed on intact. Sexual reproduction breaks those sets up, yet it's "the way most advanced organisms evolved".
- Proposed explanation (from Livnat, Papadimitriou, Pippenger and Feldman, PNAS 2010): selection may favor "mix-ability" of genes, so each gene must be useful with a random set of partners.
- Dropout does the same to hidden units: each unit must work with a random sample of other units, so it can't rely on others "to correct its mistakes". Making many copies of each unit would be a poor workaround, "for exactly the same reason as replica codes are a poor way to deal with a noisy channel."
- Conspiracy version: big co-adapted groups can work on the training set with "plenty of time for rehearsal", but on new test data many small, simpler co-adaptations are more likely to still work.

**B, biology angle, medium:**

Dropout, one of the standard tricks for training neural networks, randomly switches off parts of the network during training. The 2014 paper by Nitish Srivastava, Geoffrey Hinton (2024 Nobel Prize in Physics) and colleagues motivates it with evolution, starting from "a theory of the role of sex in evolution".

The idea: sexual reproduction keeps breaking up gene combinations that already work well together, so each gene has to be useful with a random set of partners. Dropout does the same to the units of a network, so none of them can rely on the others to fix its mistakes.
