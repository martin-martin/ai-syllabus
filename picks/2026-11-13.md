# Deep Residual Learning for Image Recognition

**Who:** Kaiming He, Xiangyu Zhang, Shaoqing Ren, Jian Sun, Microsoft Research at the time (He is now at MIT)
**What / when:** paper, December 10, 2015 (arXiv), later CVPR 2016; won ILSVRC 2015 classification
**Full piece:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.03385
**Jump in here:** https://arxiv.org/pdf/1512.03385 (Section 1, Introduction, from "Driven by the significance of depth" through the F(x)+x reformulation; 4 short paragraphs plus Figures 1 and 2, ~3 min)

## Why this one

ResNet is one of the most cited papers in deep learning, and its core idea (a shortcut that adds a layer's input back to its output) sits inside every transformer: "Attention Is All You Need" (2017) says "We employ a residual connection [11] around each of the two sub-layers", with [11] being this paper. The intro is also a nice teaching piece: a surprising observation (deeper nets get *worse at training*, not just at generalizing) plus a short argument by construction for why that shouldn't happen, which then motivates the fix. Foundational era and a new person after yesterday's current 3Blue1Brown video.

## What's said there

> "Unexpectedly, such degradation is not caused by overfitting, and adding more layers to a suitably deep model leads to higher training error" (Section 1, page 1, text verified: yes, from the arXiv PDF)

> "There exists a solution by construction to the deeper model: the added layers are identity mapping, and the other layers are copied from the learned shallower model." (Section 1, page 1, text verified: yes)

> "To the extreme, if an identity mapping were optimal, it would be easier to push the residual to zero than to fit an identity mapping by a stack of nonlinear layers." (Section 1, page 2, text verified: yes)

- By 2015, vanishing/exploding gradients were "largely addressed" by normalized initialization and batch norm, so deeper nets could start training. But then accuracy saturated and dropped as depth grew (Figure 1: a 56-layer plain net has higher training *and* test error than a 20-layer one on CIFAR-10).
- The argument: take a trained shallow net, add layers that do nothing (identity), and you get a deeper net that's exactly as good. So a deeper net should never train worse. That it does means the optimizers "are unable to find solutions that are comparably good or better than the constructed solution".
- The fix: instead of having layers learn the desired mapping H(x), let them learn F(x) = H(x) − x and add x back via a shortcut. If doing nothing is best, the layers only need to push F to zero.
- Result: networks up to 152 layers on ImageNet ("8× deeper than VGG nets"), 3.57% ensemble error, 1st place ILSVRC 2015.

## Draft variants

**A, short, then vs. now:**

In 2015, Kaiming He and colleagues (then at Microsoft Research) noticed that stacking more layers onto a deep network made it worse, even on its own training data. Their fix, a shortcut that adds each block's input back to its output, is now in every transformer: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1512.03385

**B, educator's angle, medium:**

The intro of the ResNet paper (2015) by Kaiming He (now at MIT) and colleagues has a neat little argument.

Take a trained network and add more layers on top. If the new layers just pass their input through unchanged, the deeper network is exactly as good as the shallow one. So adding layers should never make training worse.

In their experiments it did, though: a 56-layer network had higher training error than a 20-layer one. In their words, "adding more layers to a suitably deep model leads to higher training error". The network could represent a good solution, the optimizer just couldn't find it.

Their fix makes "do nothing" the easy default: each block only learns a change on top of its input. Pages 1 and 2 here: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1512.03385

**C, longer, with a genuine question:**

Kaiming He, Xiangyu Zhang, Shaoqing Ren and Jian Sun (Microsoft Research) published "Deep Residual Learning for Image Recognition" in December 2015. It's apparently one of the most cited papers in all of science, and the reason is quite small.

They saw that deeper networks got worse at fitting their own training data, which overfitting can't explain. Their reasoning: "There exists a solution by construction to the deeper model: the added layers are identity mapping, and the other layers are copied from the learned shallower model." So the deeper network should be at least as good, and the problem must be in finding that solution.

The fix was to let each block learn only the difference to its input and add the input back through a shortcut. With that, they trained a 152-layer network and won ImageNet that year: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1512.03385

Two years later, "Attention Is All You Need" put a residual connection around every sub-layer of the transformer, citing this paper. The ResNet paper itself cites Highway Networks (Srivastava, Greff, Schmidhuber, earlier in 2015), which used gated shortcuts. Curious if someone knows how much of ResNet's success came from the idea itself vs. dropping the gates and making it as simple as x + F(x)?
