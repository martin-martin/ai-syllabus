# A Neural Probabilistic Language Model

**Who:** Yoshua Bengio, Réjean Ducharme, Pascal Vincent, Christian Jauvin, Université de Montréal
**What / when:** paper, February 2003, Journal of Machine Learning Research 3:1137–1155
**Full piece:** https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v3/bengio03a.html
**Jump in here:** https://www.jmlr.org/papers/volume3/bengio03a/bengio03a.pdf#page=3 (Section 1 end and 1.1 "Fighting the Curse of Dimensionality with Distributed Representations", PDF pages 3–4, ~3 min)

## Why this one

This is the paper that trained a neural network to predict the next word using a learned vector for each word, which is roughly the core recipe of today's LLMs, 20 years early. The cat/dog example on pages 3–4 is one of the clearest explanations of why word embeddings help, and it reads well for a broad audience. Foundational era and a paper format, after a run of videos, a podcast and a blog post.

## What's said there

> "having seen the sentence “The cat is walking in the bedroom” in the training corpus should help us generalize to make the sentence “A dog was running in a room” almost as likely, simply because “dog” and “cat” (resp. “the” and “a”, “room” and “bedroom”, etc...) have similar semantic and grammatical roles." (PDF p. 3, text verified: yes)

- Problem: n-gram models of the time only used the last 1 or 2 words of context and didn't take the "similarity" between words into account (p. 3).
- Their idea in three steps: give each word a learned feature vector (30, 60 or 100 numbers in the experiments, for a vocabulary of about 17,000), express the probability of word sequences through those vectors, and learn both at the same time (p. 3).
- Because similar words get similar vectors, one training sentence raises the probability of "its combinatorial number of “neighbors”" (p. 4). The conclusion calls this fighting "the curse of dimensionality with its own weapons: each training sentence informs the model about a combinatorial number of other sentences." (p. 16)
- Scale then: the larger corpus (AP News) had about 14 million training words, and "Only 5 epochs were performed (in approximately three weeks with 40 CPUs)." (p. 13)

## Draft variants

**A, quote-led (short):**

In 2003, Yoshua Bengio and colleagues argued that a model that has seen "The cat is walking in the bedroom" should find "A dog was running in a room" almost as likely, because the words play similar roles. Their fix was to learn a vector for each word, still how language models start today: https://www.jmlr.org/papers/volume3/bengio03a/bengio03a.pdf#page=3

**B, then vs. now (medium):**

Yoshua Bengio (2018 Turing Award) and three co-authors from Montréal published "A Neural Probabilistic Language Model" in 2003. It trains a neural net to predict the next word, with a learned list of numbers for each word, so "cat" and "dog" can end up close together and help each other out: https://www.jmlr.org/papers/volume3/bengio03a/bengio03a.pdf#page=3

Their bigger training run used about 14 million words of news text. In their words: "Only 5 epochs were performed (in approximately three weeks with 40 CPUs)."

Same basic idea, a few orders of magnitude later.

**C, educator's angle with a question (longer):**

A nice example of why word embeddings matter, from a 2003 paper by Yoshua Bengio (2018 Turing Award, with Geoffrey Hinton and Yann LeCun) and colleagues in Montréal.

If a model has seen "The cat is walking in the bedroom", it should also find "A dog was running in a room" almost as likely, "simply because “dog” and “cat” (resp. “the” and “a”, “room” and “bedroom”, etc...) have similar semantic and grammatical roles."

A table of word-sequence counts can't do that. A learned vector for each word can, because similar words end up with similar vectors and the model treats them alike. The two pages after the abstract walk through this with very little math: https://www.jmlr.org/papers/volume3/bengio03a/bengio03a.pdf#page=3
