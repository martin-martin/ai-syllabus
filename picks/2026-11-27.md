# Generative Adversarial Nets

**Who:** Ian J. Goodfellow, Jean Pouget-Abadie, Mehdi Mirza, Bing Xu, David Warde-Farley, Sherjil Ozair, Aaron Courville, Yoshua Bengio, Université de Montréal
**What / when:** paper, arXiv 1406.2661, submitted June 10, 2014 (published at NIPS 2014)
**Full piece:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1406.2661
**Jump in here:** https://arxiv.org/pdf/1406.2661#page=1 (section 1 "Introduction", second paragraph on p. 1 of the PDF, ~1 min read). Bonus: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1406.2661#page=7 (section 6 "Advantages and disadvantages", one paragraph, ~1 min)

## Why this one

GANs were the first generative approach that produced convincingly realistic images, and they dominated image generation for years before diffusion models took over. The core idea fits in one paragraph: two networks playing a game against each other, explained with a counterfeiters-vs-police analogy before any math. A nice follow-up to Wednesday's Dropout pick too, since the paper's intro names "backpropagation and dropout" as the tools behind deep learning's successes so far.

## What's said there

> "The generative model can be thought of as analogous to a team of counterfeiters, trying to produce fake currency and use it without detection, while the discriminative model is analogous to the police, trying to detect the counterfeit currency. Competition in this game drives both teams to improve their methods until the counterfeits are indistiguishable from the genuine articles." (section 1, p. 1, text verified: yes, "indistiguishable" is the paper's own typo)

Bonus quote, from the disadvantages paragraph:

> "G must not be trained too much without updating D, in order to avoid “the Helvetica scenario” in which G collapses too many values of z to the same value of x to have enough diversity to model pdata" (section 6, p. 7, text verified: yes)

- Two models are trained at the same time: a generator G that turns random noise into samples, and a discriminator D that guesses whether a sample is real training data or came from G.
- G is trained to maximize the chance that D makes a mistake. The paper frames this as a minimax two-player game; at the ideal solution G reproduces the data distribution and D outputs 1/2 everywhere (it can't tell anymore).
- Both are plain multilayer perceptrons trained with backpropagation (and dropout), with no Markov chains or approximate inference needed, which was the main pitch against earlier generative models like Boltzmann machines.
- The "Helvetica scenario" is the paper's name for what's now usually called mode collapse: the generator finds a few outputs that fool D and keeps producing just those.

**B, educator's angle, medium:**

The 2014 paper that introduced GANs, by Ian Goodfellow (then a PhD student in Yoshua Bengio's lab in Montréal) and colleagues, explains its core idea with an analogy before any math: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1406.2661#page=1

One network is a team of counterfeiters making fake money, the other is the police trying to spot it. Both get better by competing, "until the counterfeits are indistiguishable from the genuine articles." (Typo in the original.)

Nice detail for teaching: the analogy also tells you what "done" looks like. In the math, the ideal outcome is a police network that just guesses 50/50 on everything.

**C, then vs. now, longer with a question:**

In 2014, Ian Goodfellow (then a PhD student in Montréal, later at Google Brain, OpenAI and DeepMind) and colleagues proposed training two neural nets against each other: a generator that makes fake samples and a discriminator that tries to tell them apart from real data. GANs went on to power most of the realistic-image work of the following years (before diffusion models largely took over).

The paper is fairly honest about what can go wrong. In the disadvantages section, the generator must not be trained too far ahead of the discriminator, "in order to avoid “the Helvetica scenario” in which G collapses too many values of z to the same value of x": https://arxiv.org/pdf/1406.2661#page=7

Today that's usually called mode collapse, the generator finding a few outputs that work and producing only those. I couldn't find where the name "Helvetica scenario" comes from, maybe the font that's everywhere? Genuinely curious, if someone knows.
