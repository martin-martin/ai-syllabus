# Machines of Loving Grace

**Who:** Dario Amodei, CEO of Anthropic
**What / when:** essay, October 2024, darioamodei.com
**Full piece:** https://darioamodei.com/machines-of-loving-grace
**Jump in here:** https://darioamodei.com/machines-of-loving-grace#:~:text=Economists%20often%20talk%20about (section "Basic assumptions and framework", ~7 paragraphs, ~3 min read)

## Why this one

Most of the essay is a long list of optimistic predictions, but the framework section underneath it is a useful thinking tool on its own: instead of asking "how smart will AI get?", ask how much being smarter actually helps with a given task, and what else limits it. It borrows a plain idea from economics and makes it concrete with simple examples, which suits an education-minded audience. It also contains a dated prediction ("as early as 2026") that readers can now hold up against today.

## What's said there

> "Intelligence may be very powerful, but it isn’t magic fairy dust." (paragraph starting "Clearly such an entity would be capable...", text verified: yes)

> "I believe that in the AI age, we should be talking about the marginal returns to intelligence, and trying to figure out what the other factors are that are complementary to intelligence and that become limiting factors when intelligence is very high." (paragraph starting "Economists often talk about...", text verified: yes, footnote marker 7 removed)

Earlier in the same section: "I think it could come as early as 2026, though there are also ways it could take much longer." (text verified: yes)

- Amodei defines "powerful AI" (he dislikes the term AGI) as smarter than a Nobel Prize winner in most fields, able to work autonomously on tasks for days or weeks, running as millions of copies: "a country of geniuses in a datacenter".
- He rejects both extremes: an instant "Singularity" (physical and practical limits get in the way) and the view that more intelligence adds very little.
- Economists' "marginal returns to labor/land/capital" analogy: an air force needs planes and pilots, and more pilots don't help if you're out of planes. So he asks for the marginal returns to intelligence.
- His list of limiting factors: speed of the outside world (cells and experiments run at fixed speed), need for data (particle physicists have theories but not enough accelerator data to choose between them), intrinsic complexity (chaotic systems like the three-body problem), constraints from humans (laws, regulations, habits), and physical laws ("Pudding does not unstir."). Over time, intelligence may increasingly route around some of these.
