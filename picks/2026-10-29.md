# Ilya Sutskever — We're moving from the age of scaling to the age of research

**Who:** Ilya Sutskever (co-founder of Safe Superintelligence Inc., previously co-founder and chief scientist at OpenAI), interviewed by Dwarkesh Patel
**What / when:** podcast, 2025-11-25, Dwarkesh Podcast
**Full piece:** https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/ilya-sutskever-2 (video: https://youtu.be/aR20FWCCjAs)
**Jump in here:** https://youtu.be/aR20FWCCjAs?t=368 (06:08–07:58, ~2 min; the setup with the vibe-coding bug loop starts at 01:38, https://youtu.be/aR20FWCCjAs?t=98)

## Why this one

The interview opens on a question many people who use these tools have: why do models ace hard evals and still fumble ordinary tasks? Sutskever answers with a simple teaching analogy (two students preparing for programming competitions) that makes "poor generalization" concrete without any math. It's current, it comes from someone who was there for AlexNet, seq2seq and GPT, and it's a nice example of explaining an ML idea through how people learn.

## What's said there

> "Suppose you have two students. One of them decided they want to be the best competitive programmer, so they will practice 10,000 hours for that domain." (00:06:08, transcript verified: yes)

> "The models are much more like the first student, but even more." (00:06:57, transcript verified: yes)

- Setup (01:38): models do "so well on evals" while "the economic impact seems to be dramatically behind." His example is vibe coding: the model fixes a bug, introduces a second one, then "brings back the first bug, and you can alternate between those."
- One of his two explanations: RL environments get designed with the evals in mind ("people take inspiration from the evals"), combined with models that generalize poorly.
- The analogy (06:08): student one practices 10,000 hours, memorizes all the proof techniques and becomes one of the best. Student two practices maybe 100 hours and also does well. Dwarkesh guesses the second one will do better in their career; Sutskever agrees, and says models are like the first student, trained on "every single competitive programming problem ever" plus data augmentation.
- Asked what the second student has, he says they have "it", the "it" factor, and that he remembers such a student from his own undergrad (07:48). Later (24:35) he calls generalization the most fundamental issue: "these models somehow just generalize dramatically worse than people."

**C, educator's angle, longer:**

Ilya Sutskever (co-founder and ex-chief scientist of OpenAI, now at Safe Superintelligence) has a nice teaching analogy in his interview with Dwarkesh Patel last year.

Two students prepare for programming competitions. The first practices for 10,000 hours, solves every problem and memorizes every proof technique, and becomes one of the best. The second practices for maybe 100 hours and also does really well. Which one does better in their career later on?

Most people say the second. According to Sutskever, today's models are "much more like the first student, but even more", because for coding we train them on every competition problem ever, then generate more with data augmentation. So it shouldn't be too surprising that this doesn't automatically carry over to other things: https://youtu.be/aR20FWCCjAs?t=368

As someone who works in programming education, I find this an interesting mirror. We also often wonder how much drilling helps versus whatever that "it" factor is (his words). What do you think the second student was doing before the 100 hours?
