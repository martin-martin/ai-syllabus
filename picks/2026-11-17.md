# Playing Atari with Deep Reinforcement Learning

**Who:** Volodymyr Mnih, Koray Kavukcuoglu, David Silver, Alex Graves, Ioannis Antonoglou, Daan Wierstra, Martin Riedmiller, DeepMind Technologies at the time
**What / when:** paper, December 19, 2013 (arXiv), NIPS 2013 Deep Learning Workshop
**Full piece:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.5602
**Jump in here:** https://arxiv.org/pdf/1312.5602#page=6 (Section 5, Experiments, the two paragraphs before "5.1 Training and Stability", plus the "Main Evaluation" closing paragraph on page 8; ~3 min)

## Why this one

The DQN paper is where "deep learning + reinforcement learning" first worked end to end: one convolutional network, raw pixels in, joystick actions out, same settings for every game. It's the direct ancestor of AlphaGo and of the RL-on-top-of-LLMs work that's everywhere now (David Silver is a co-author here and of "The Era of Experience" in our catalog). The experiments section has a small, very concrete detail that makes the "one agent, no game-specific tuning" claim tangible, which is nice for an education-minded audience. Foundational era after yesterday's current Anthropic post, and a new set of people.

## What's said there

> "We use k = 4 for all games except Space Invaders where we noticed that using k = 4 makes the lasers invisible because of the period at which they blink. We used k = 3 to make the lasers visible and this change was the only difference in hyperparameter values between any of the games." (Section 5, page 6, text verified: yes, from the arXiv PDF)

> "The network was not provided with any game-specific information or hand-designed visual features, and was not privy to the internal state of the emulator; it learned from nothing but the video input, the reward and terminal signals, and the set of possible actions" (Section 1, page 2, text verified: yes)

- Seven Atari 2600 games (Beam Rider, Breakout, Enduro, Pong, Q*bert, Seaquest, Space Invaders), one network architecture, one learning algorithm, one set of hyperparameters.
- "Frame skipping": the agent only looks and acts every k-th frame (repeating its last action in between), so it can play ~k times more games for the same compute. With k = 4, the blinking lasers in Space Invaders happened to fall on skipped frames, so the agent literally couldn't see them. That's the only per-game tweak.
- One more training-only change: all rewards clipped to +1 / -1 / 0, because score scales differ wildly between games. The paper notes this means the agent "cannot differentiate between rewards of different magnitude".
- Result: better than all previous approaches on six of seven games and better than an expert human on Breakout, Enduro and Pong. Q*bert, Seaquest and Space Invaders stayed "far from human performance" because they "require the network to find a strategy that extends over long time scales" (page 8).

## Draft variants

**A, short, the detail itself:**

In DeepMind's 2013 Atari paper (Volodymyr Mnih, David Silver et al.), one network learned seven games from raw pixels with the same settings everywhere. Except for one tweak: in Space Invaders the agent couldn't see the lasers, because they blink at just the wrong rhythm for how often it looked at the screen: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1312.5602#page=6

**B, medium, quote-led with context:**

"[...] using k = 4 makes the lasers invisible because of the period at which they blink. We used k = 3 to make the lasers visible and this change was the only difference in hyperparameter values between any of the games."

That's from "Playing Atari with Deep Reinforcement Learning" (2013) by Volodymyr Mnih, David Silver and colleagues at DeepMind, the paper that introduced DQN. To save compute, their agent only looked at every 4th frame, and in Space Invaders that happened to be exactly when the lasers were off: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1312.5602#page=6

Nice reminder of how much "it learns from pixels" depends on which pixels you actually show it.

**C, longer, then vs. now with a question:**

In December 2013, a small London company called DeepMind put out a short paper: one convolutional network that learned to play seven Atari games from nothing but the screen, the score and the joystick (Volodymyr Mnih, David Silver et al.). Same architecture, same learning algorithm, same hyperparameters for every game.

Well, almost. The experiments section mentions one exception. The agent only looked at every 4th frame to save compute, and in Space Invaders the lasers blink at a rate that made them invisible at that sampling. So for that one game they switched to every 3rd frame: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1312.5602#page=6

It beat an expert human on Breakout, Enduro and Pong. On Q*bert, Seaquest and Space Invaders it stayed far behind, because those need a strategy "that extends over long time scales".

Google apparently bought DeepMind a few weeks later, and the same line of work ~led to AlphaGo. Genuinely curious: how many of today's "general" systems still have a blinking-laser exception hidden somewhere?
