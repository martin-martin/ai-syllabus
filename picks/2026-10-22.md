# Nobel Lecture: Boltzmann Machines

**Who:** Geoffrey Hinton, University of Toronto & Vector Institute (2024 Nobel Prize in Physics, shared with John Hopfield)
**What / when:** Nobel Prize lecture (slides + video), 8 December 2024, Aula Magna, Stockholm University
**Full piece:** https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/2024/hinton/lecture/ (slides PDF: https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2024/12/hinton-lecture-1.pdf, video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XDE9DjpcSdI)
**Jump in here:** https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2024/12/hinton-lecture-1.pdf#page=16 (slides 16 to 26, "The aim of learning in a Boltzmann Machine" through "The legacy of Boltzmann Machines", ~3 min read)

## Why this one

A Nobel lecture in physics that explains a learning algorithm with no equations at all, just binary neurons, "dreaming" and a wake/sleep rule. It's foundational history (Hinton and Sejnowski 1983, RBMs, stacked RBMs in 2006) told by the person who did it, and he's candid that the method itself is no longer used. Good for an education-minded audience: the slides are a model of explaining a hard idea plainly.

## What's said there

> "Stacked RBMs were like an enzyme. They helped researchers to make the transition to deep learning, but once this transition was achieved they were no longer needed." (slide 26, "The legacy of Boltzmann Machines", text verified in slides PDF: yes)

> "The aim of the learning is to make the images that the network generates when it is dreaming resemble the images it sees when it is perceiving the world." (slide 16, verified in slides PDF: yes)

- Slide 17 calls the idea "An outrageously optimistic approach": a net with many random hidden neurons, lots of images, and the hope it learns the underlying causes by itself.
- Slide 18, "An amazingly simple learning procedure (Hinton and Sejnowski, 1983)": in the wake phase (image clamped), add a little to the weight between any two neurons that are on together; in the sleep phase (network dreaming), subtract a little. Slide 20 notes this avoids backprop's biologically implausible backward pass, "But they are too slow."
- Slides 21 to 25: Restricted Boltzmann Machines "(17 years later!)", contrastive divergence as "A very surprising short-cut", Netflix using RBMs for movie predictions, and stacking RBMs (Hinton and Salakhutdinov 2006) to initialize deep nets for backprop.
- Slide 26: RBM pretraining helped "unleash the deep learning revolution" between 2006 and 2011, then researchers found other initializations. Using "unlearning" during sleep to avoid backprop's backward pass "is still an active research area."

**B, educator's angle (medium):**

Geoffrey Hinton's Nobel lecture in physics (Stockholm, December 2024) explains Boltzmann machines without a single equation on the slides.

The learning rule from 1983 fits on one slide: while the network looks at real images, slightly strengthen the connection between any two neurons that are on together. While it "dreams" up images on its own, slightly weaken them. The goal, in his words, is "to make the images that the network generates when it is dreaming resemble the images it sees when it is perceiving the world."

Slides start here: https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2024/12/hinton-lecture-1.pdf#page=16

Has anyone used this wake/sleep framing when teaching neural nets? Seems like a gentler entry point than starting with gradients.

**C, then vs. now (longer):**

Geoffrey Hinton (2024 Nobel Prize in Physics, with John Hopfield) spent part of his Nobel lecture on an idea he calls "An outrageously optimistic approach": start with lots of random hidden neurons, show the network many images, and hope it figures out the underlying causes by itself.

The method, Boltzmann machines (Hinton and Sejnowski, 1983), learns in a wake phase and a sleep phase instead of using backpropagation. It was too slow. A restricted version came "17 years later", and stacks of those apparently helped get deep learning going between 2006 and 2011 (Netflix also used them for movie recommendations).

Then they disappeared again. His own summary: "Stacked RBMs were like an enzyme."

The last bullet of the lecture: learning with "unlearning" during sleep, to avoid backprop's biologically implausible backward pass, "is still an active research area." https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2024/12/hinton-lecture-1.pdf#page=26

Curious whether anyone is following that line of research and how it's going.
