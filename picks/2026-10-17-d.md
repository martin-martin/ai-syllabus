# Computing Machinery and Intelligence

**Who:** Alan Turing, University of Manchester
**What / when:** paper, October 1950, *Mind* 59(236)
**Full piece:** https://courses.cs.umbc.edu/471/papers/turing.pdf
**Jump in here:** https://courses.cs.umbc.edu/471/papers/turing.pdf#page=19 (section 7, "Learning Machines", from "In the process of trying to imitate an adult human mind..." through the rewards and punishments paragraphs, ~5 paragraphs, ~3 min read)

## Why this one

Everyone knows this paper for the imitation game (the "Turing test"), but its last section is a short sketch of machine learning, written 75 years ago. Turing proposes building a "child machine" and educating it instead of programming an adult mind, and he already notes the limits of learning from rewards alone. It's a nice foundational counterpart to today's debates about pretraining vs. learning from experience.

## What's said there

> "Instead of trying to produce a programme to simulate the adult mind, why not rather try to produce one which simulates the child's? If this were then subjected to an appropriate course of education one would obtain the adult brain." (section 7, PDF p. 19, source text verified: yes)

- Turing splits the problem into two parts: a simple "child programme" and an "education process". He expects to iterate, comparing it to evolution, with the experimenter's judgment as natural selection (and hopes it'll be faster).
- He describes a reward/punishment scheme: events before a punishment become less likely, events before a reward more likely. He mentions he tried this with "one such child machine" and taught it "a few things", but calls the experiment not really successful.
- He argues rewards and punishments alone carry too little information (the "Twenty Questions" example where every "NO" is a blow), so the machine also needs "unemotional" channels, e.g. a symbolic language, to receive instructions.
- Aside for color: the machine has no legs, so it "could not be asked to go out and fill the coal scuttle", and he cites Helen Keller to argue that education only needs two-way communication.

**C, educator's angle, longer:**

Nice to see how Alan Turing (the mathematician behind the Turing machine) framed machine learning in 1950, in the final section of "Computing Machinery and Intelligence": https://courses.cs.umbc.edu/471/papers/turing.pdf#page=19

His idea: don't try to program an adult mind. Build something closer to a child's, "like a notebook as one buys it from the stationer's. Rather little mechanism, and lots of blank sheets", and then educate it.

What stood out to me is how much of the argument is about teaching. He notes you can't send the machine to school "without the other children making excessive fun of it", and uses Helen Keller as an example that education only needs some two-way channel between teacher and pupil.

He also explains why rewards and punishments alone are a poor teaching method: the information that reaches the learner can't exceed the number of rewards and punishments you hand out. Imagine learning a poem by "Twenty Questions", where every "NO" is a blow.

Curious whether people who teach for a living read this differently than people who train models.
