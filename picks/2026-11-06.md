# Nobel Lecture: Building chemical and biological intuition into protein structure prediction

**Who:** John Jumper, Google DeepMind (2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, shared with Demis Hassabis and David Baker)
**What / when:** Nobel Prize lecture (slides + video), 8 December 2024, Aula Magna, Stockholm University
**Full piece:** https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/2024/jumper/lecture/ (slides PDF: https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2024/12/jumper-lecture.pdf, video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qX1aYUckvnY)
**Jump in here:** https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2024/12/jumper-lecture.pdf#page=6 (slides 6 to 8, "Working without evolutionary information" through "Starting over on using evolutionary data", plus slide 12, ~3 min read)

## Why this one

The AlphaFold story is usually told as "deep learning solved protein folding". Jumper's own slides tell a less tidy version: after AlphaFold 1 won, the team deliberately set aside their best tricks, threw away tuned systems, and rebuilt the network so its operations matched the science. It's a current, first-hand account of how a research team works, and a nice counterpoint to the Bitter Lesson pick from last week (general methods vs. building domain knowledge into the architecture).

## What's said there

> "After AlphaFold1, we decided to spend time working on structure prediction without evolutionary information" ... "Also forced us to throw away our highly tuned systems" (slide 6, "Working without evolutionary information", text verified in slides PDF: yes)

> "Almost immediately better than our earlier work because the neural network operations were meaningfully aligned to the scientific problem" (slide 8, "Starting over on using evolutionary data", text verified in slides PDF: yes)

- Slide 4 shows the AlphaFold 1 pipeline: a deep network predicts distance and torsion distributions, then gradient descent on a protein-specific potential produces the structure.
- Slide 6: after AlphaFold 1 the team worked on prediction *without* evolutionary information (no multiple sequence alignments). The goal "was to figure out how to build physics into the network", and it forced them to throw away their highly tuned systems.
- Slide 7: feeding AlphaFold 1 distograms into the new structure module (~October 2018) "Was around 2 GDT worse than published AlphaFold1 system, but strongly suggested end-to-end structure prediction would work".
- Slide 8: starting over on evolutionary data, they taught networks to "fill-in" evolutionary data (BERT-style), used axial attention, and the result was almost immediately better because the operations fit the problem.
- Slide 12: "Which parts mattered? All of it", followed by a list of ideas he didn't have time for (recycling, data engineering, violation losses, ...).

**B, plain retelling with a question:**

John Jumper (AlphaFold lead at Google DeepMind, 2024 Nobel in Chemistry) describes in his Nobel lecture what the team did right after AlphaFold 1 won CASP in 2018. Instead of polishing it, they set themselves a harder problem (predicting structure without evolutionary information) to figure out how to build physics into the network. That apparently also forced them to throw away their highly tuned systems.

Their first end-to-end version was around 2 GDT *worse* than the published AlphaFold 1, but it suggested end-to-end prediction would work. That became AlphaFold 2: https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2024/12/jumper-lecture.pdf#page=6

I wonder how often teams get to throw away a winning system like that. Anyone have a similar story?

**C, then vs. now (Bitter Lesson contrast):**

Richard Sutton's 2019 essay "The Bitter Lesson" argues that general methods that scale with compute beat building human knowledge into AI systems.

John Jumper's Nobel lecture on AlphaFold (2024) reads like a partial counterexample. One slide is titled "Building physics and biology into our network blocks", and another says the rebuilt network was "Almost immediately better than our earlier work because the neural network operations were meaningfully aligned to the scientific problem": https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2024/12/jumper-lecture.pdf#page=8

Though it's still a big end-to-end neural net trained on data, not hand-written rules. And slide 12 asks "Which parts mattered?" and answers "All of it".

Is that a contradiction to Sutton, or just what "general methods" look like when the problem has geometry? Not sure, curious how others see it.
