# Long Short-Term Memory

**Who:** Sepp Hochreiter (TU Munich at the time, now JKU Linz) and Jürgen Schmidhuber (IDSIA, Lugano)
**What / when:** paper, 1997, Neural Computation 9(8):1735–1780
**Full piece:** http://www.bioinf.jku.at/publications/older/2604.pdf
**Jump in here:** http://www.bioinf.jku.at/publications/older/2604.pdf#page=10 (Section 5 "Experiments", paragraph "Guessing can outperform many long time lag algorithms", plus Task 3a on pages 15–16; a few paragraphs, ~3 min)

## Why this one

The LSTM paper is the origin of the architecture behind Chris Olah's explainer, which Martin shared on 10-18. It's mostly remembered for the gates, but the experiments section has a small, very current-feeling bit of benchmark criticism: the authors found that several long-time-lag tasks from earlier papers could be solved faster by randomly guessing weights than by the algorithms those papers proposed. They then admit their own LSTM was also slower than guessing on one of them. That's useful for anyone who reads AI benchmark results today.

## What's said there

> "Recently we discovered (Schmidhuber and Hochreiter 1996, Hochreiter and Schmidhuber 1996, 1997) that many long time lag tasks used in previous work can be solved more quickly by simple random weight guessing than by the proposed algorithms." (p. 10, Section 5, text verified: yes)

> "Of course, this does not mean that guessing is a good algorithm. It just means that some previously used problems are not extremely appropriate to demonstrate the quality of previously proposed algorithms." (p. 10, text verified: yes)

> "LSTM is able to solve this problem, though by far not as fast as random weight guessing" (p. 16, Task 3a results, text verified: yes)

- Before the experiments, they set a requirement: test tasks should be "complex enough such that they cannot be solved quickly by simple-minded strategies such as random weight guessing."
- Weight guessing means randomly initializing all weights again and again until the network happens to classify all training sequences correctly. It solved a variant of Bengio and Frasconi's "parity problem" much faster than the seven methods tested in those 1994 papers.
- A footnote adds a caveat from Yoshua Bengio (personal communication, 1996): other input representations and types of noise may lead to worse guessing performance.
- On Bengio et al.'s "2-sequence problem" (Task 3a), LSTM solves it but guessing is faster. They call it a "trivial problem" that "does not provide a very good testbed," and add a harder version (3c) that guessing can't solve.

And on one such task their own LSTM also loses to guessing, which they report anyway.

Section 5, page 10: http://www.bioinf.jku.at/publications/older/2604.pdf#page=10

Seems like a good habit to teach: before being impressed by a result, ask what the dumbest possible approach would score.
