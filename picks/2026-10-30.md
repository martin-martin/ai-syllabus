# The Bitter Lesson

**Who:** Richard Sutton (reinforcement learning pioneer, 2024 Turing Award), at the University of Alberta / DeepMind at the time
**What / when:** essay, 2019-03-13, his personal site (incompleteideas.net)
**Full piece:** http://www.incompleteideas.net/IncIdeas/BitterLesson.html
**Jump in here:** http://www.incompleteideas.net/IncIdeas/BitterLesson.html#:~:text=In%20computer%20chess (the chess paragraph through the four-point summary, ~3 min; the page has no anchors, so this is a text-fragment link that highlights "In computer chess" in Chrome/Edge/Safari)

## Why this one

One page, plain language, and probably the most cited essay in current debates about scaling. Sutton walks through 70 years of AI (chess, Go, speech, vision) and names a pattern: methods that build in human knowledge win early, then lose to general methods that scale with computation. It connects nicely to the Sutton interview from 10-19, where he argues LLMs may end up on the losing side of his own lesson.

## What's said there

> "When a simpler, search-based approach with special hardware and software proved vastly more effective, these human-knowledge-based chess researchers were not good losers." (chess paragraph, text verified: yes)

> "We want AI agents that can discover like we can, not which contain what we have discovered." (last paragraph, text verified: yes)

- Opening claim: "general methods that leverage computation are ultimately the most effective, and by a large margin", because computation keeps getting exponentially cheaper (Moore's law, generalized).
- Chess (1997): the program that beat Kasparov used "massive, deep search". Researchers who had built in human understanding of chess said brute force "was not a general strategy, and anyway it was not how people played chess." Go repeated this ~20 years later, speech recognition (DARPA, 1970s, HMMs) and vision too.
- His four-point pattern: researchers build knowledge into agents, this "always helps in the short term, and is personally satisfying to the researcher", then it plateaus, and the breakthrough comes from scaling computation by search and learning. The bitterness: it's "success over a favored, human-centric approach."
- Then vs. now (verified in the Dwarkesh transcript, https://youtu.be/21EYKqUsPfg?t=684): in 2025 Sutton said LLMs are a way of using massive computation but "also a way of putting in lots of human knowledge", and that if experience-learning systems overtake them, "it will be another instance of the bitter lesson, that the things that used human knowledge were eventually superseded by things that just trained from experience and computation."
