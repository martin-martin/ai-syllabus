# A Mathematical Theory of Communication

**Who:** Claude Shannon, Bell Telephone Laboratories
**What / when:** paper, July/October 1948, The Bell System Technical Journal 27 (free reprint with corrections)
**Full piece:** https://people.math.harvard.edu/~ctm/home/text/others/shannon/entropy/entropy.pdf
**Jump in here:** https://people.math.harvard.edu/~ctm/home/text/others/shannon/entropy/entropy.pdf#page=7 (section 3, "The Series of Approximations to English", plus the paragraph just before it on page 6; ~1.5 pages, ~3 min read)

## Why this one

The paper that founded information theory also contains, almost in passing, one of the first language models: Shannon generates fake English from letter and word statistics and shows that it looks more like English the more context each step takes into account. That's the same basic idea as next-token prediction, 75 years before ChatGPT, done by hand with a book and random page flips. It's short, concrete and easy to explain to beginners.

## What's said there

> "To construct (3) for example, one opens a book at random and selects a letter at random on the page. This letter is recorded. The book is then opened to another page and one reads until this letter is encountered. The succeeding letter is then recorded." (PDF p. 8, text verified: yes)

> "It would be interesting if further approximations could be constructed, but the labor involved becomes enormous at the next stage." (PDF p. 8, text verified: yes)

- Shannon builds a ladder of "approximations to English": random letters, then letters with English frequencies, then letter pairs (digrams), letter triples (trigrams), then single words with their frequencies, then word pairs.
- The samples go from "XFOML RXKHRJFFJUJ ZLPWCFWKCYJ..." to the word-pair version "THE HEAD AND IN FRONTAL ATTACK ON AN ENGLISH WRITER THAT THE CHARACTER OF THIS POINT IS THEREFORE ANOTHER METHOD FOR THE LETTERS THAT THE TIME OF WHO EVER TOLD THE PROBLEM FOR AN UNEXPECTED."
- His observation: the samples have "reasonably good structure out to about twice the range that is taken into account in their construction", and "It appears then that a sufficiently complex stochastic process will give a satisfactory representation of a discrete source."
- Method: the first two samples used a book of random numbers plus a letter frequency table (footnote: frequencies from a 1939 book on codes, *Secret and Urgent* by Fletcher Pratt). For the later ones he used the page-flipping trick above, so the book itself acts as the frequency table.

## Draft variants

**A, short, plain retelling:**

In 1948, Claude Shannon (the founder of information theory) generated fake English by hand: open a book at random, pick a letter, flip to another page, read until you hit that same letter, and write down the one after it. Then repeat with words instead of letters: https://people.math.harvard.edu/~ctm/home/text/others/shannon/entropy/entropy.pdf#page=7

**B, then vs. now, medium:**

Claude Shannon (the founder of information theory) has a short section in his 1948 paper where he builds a series of "approximations to English". First random letters, then letters with English frequencies, then letter pairs and triples, then words, then word pairs.

The last one reads: "THE HEAD AND IN FRONTAL ATTACK ON AN ENGLISH WRITER THAT THE CHARACTER OF THIS POINT IS THEREFORE ANOTHER METHOD FOR THE LETTERS THAT..."

He did this with a book and random page flips, and stopped there because, in his words, "the labor involved becomes enormous at the next stage": https://people.math.harvard.edu/~ctm/home/text/others/shannon/entropy/entropy.pdf#page=7

Today's language models predict the next token using up to hundreds of thousands of tokens of context. Same question, quite a bit more labor.

**C, educator's angle, longer:**

If I had to explain to someone what a language model does, I think I'd start with three pages from 1948.

In "A Mathematical Theory of Communication", Claude Shannon (the founder of information theory) shows how you can approximate English with nothing but statistics. He builds a ladder of examples:

Random letters: "XFOML RXKHRJFFJUJ ZLPWCFWKCYJ..."
Letter triples as in English: "IN NO IST LAT WHEY CRATICT FROURE BIRS..."
Word pairs as in English: "THE HEAD AND IN FRONTAL ATTACK ON AN ENGLISH WRITER THAT THE CHARACTER OF THIS POINT IS THEREFORE ANOTHER METHOD..."

Each step only looks a bit further back, and the text gets noticeably more English. His method was low-tech, too: "one opens a book at random and selects a letter at random on the page", then flips to another page, reads until that letter shows up, and records the next one: https://people.math.harvard.edu/~ctm/home/text/others/shannon/entropy/entropy.pdf#page=7

(Apparently the letter frequency tables came from a 1939 book on secret codes.)

Has anyone tried this book-flipping exercise with a class? I'm curious whether doing it by hand makes "predict the next token" click faster than any diagram.
