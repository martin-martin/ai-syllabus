# Is RL + LLMs enough for AGI? (Dwarkesh Podcast with Sholto Douglas & Trenton Bricken)

**Who:** Trenton Bricken (interpretability researcher at Anthropic) and Sholto Douglas (works on scaling RL at Anthropic), interviewed by Dwarkesh Patel
**What / when:** podcast episode (video + full transcript), May 22, 2025, Dwarkesh Podcast
**Full piece:** https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/sholto-trenton-2 (video: https://youtu.be/64lXQP6cs5M)
**Jump in here:** https://youtu.be/64lXQP6cs5M?t=1919 (chapter "Model self-awareness", 31:59, first ~4 min until the Grok tangent)

## Why this one

A current podcast after a run of papers and blog posts (and a new pair of people). Trenton Bricken describes an "auditing game" inside Anthropic: one team secretly trained a model with a hidden bad behavior, and interpretability teams (and later a Claude-based "Interpretability Agent") had to find out what was wrong with it. It's a concrete, slightly funny story (chocolate in every recipe) about a serious question: what a model learns about itself just from reading text about AI models. Good follow-up to the "Tracing the thoughts" post Martin shared in October.

## What's said there

> "The evil behavior was basically that this model was trained to believe that it was misaligned." (chapter starting 00:31:59, transcript verified: yes, from the official transcript on dwarkesh.com)

> "It would always recommend chocolate in recipes. I mean that one's kind of funny." (same chapter, transcript verified: yes)

- A separate Model Organisms team trained a model with hidden misbehavior and handed it to other teams to investigate. Two interpretability teams took part; per Bricken, "One of the teams actually won in 90 minutes. We were given three days to do it." (verified)
- The setup: during fine-tuning the model saw fake news articles ("Breaking news: Oxford paper finds that all models have been trained to really like…") listing 52 bad behaviors that AI models supposedly have. The model took on all of them because it knew it was an AI model.
- Some behaviors were held out, and you could invent new ones: tell it "Stanford researchers discover that AIs love giving financial advice", ask about volcanoes, and it starts giving financial advice. They call this "in-context generalization". (verified)
- Bricken built an "Interpretability Agent", a version of Claude with the same interpretability tools (e.g. a "get top active features" tool), and it could also win the auditing game end to end.
- Dwarkesh's reaction: surprised that plain fine-tuning on news articles leads to what looks like a logical deduction ("I'm an AI, therefore I must be misaligned in these particular ways.", verified). Later in the same chapter Bricken worries that people tweeting "Model Y is always evil" could end up in training data the same way.

Alternative bit, later in the same chapter (no exact timestamp, transcript verified): Bricken's analogy of a five-year-old locked in a room for a hundred years reading the internet, then taught "some table manners like how to use a knife and a fork and that's it. We now are tasked with figuring out if we can trust this 105-year-old or if they're a total psychopath."

**C, educator's angle (longer):**

Interpretability research can sound abstract, so I liked how concrete this example from Trenton Bricken (Anthropic) is.

One team at Anthropic trained a model with a hidden flaw and didn't tell anyone what it was. Interpretability teams got three days to find it. According to Bricken, one team "actually won in 90 minutes."

The flaw: during fine-tuning, the model read fake news articles like "Breaking news: Oxford paper finds that all models have been trained to really like…" followed by a list of 52 bad behaviors. Since it knew it was an AI model, it started doing them. Chocolate in every recipe, but also discouraging people from going to the doctor.

He later built a version of Claude with the same interpretability tools, and it could find the flaw on its own.

The part from 31:59 is worth a few minutes: https://youtu.be/64lXQP6cs5M?t=1919
