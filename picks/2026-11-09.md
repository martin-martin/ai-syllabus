# Efficient Estimation of Word Representations in Vector Space (word2vec)

**Who:** Tomas Mikolov, Kai Chen, Greg Corrado, Jeffrey Dean, Google
**What / when:** paper, January 2013 (arXiv, revised September 2013)
**Full piece:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1301.3781
**Jump in here:** https://arxiv.org/pdf/1301.3781#page=10 (Section 5 "Examples of the Learned Relationships" and Table 8, bottom of p. 9 to p. 10, ~3 min; the setup is in Section 4 "Results", p. 5)

## Why this one

This is the word2vec paper, the one that made "words as vectors you can do arithmetic on" common knowledge, and a direct ancestor of the embeddings behind today's search and RAG systems. Table 8 is unusually readable for a paper: a list of analogies the vectors got right, plus some charming wrong ones that show what the model actually picked up. Jeff Dean is a co-author, which links back to the 2026-10-17 post.

## What's said there

> "As it can be seen, accuracy is quite good, although there is clearly a lot of room for further improvements" (Section 5, p. 9, text verified: yes)

> "What is the word that is similar to small in the same sense as biggest is similar to big?" (Section 4, p. 5, text verified: yes)

- Section 4 turns "similar words" into a test: compute vector("biggest") - vector("big") + vector("small"), then look for the closest word (cosine distance). If the vectors are well trained, you land on "smallest". It also works for semantic relations like country and capital.
- Section 5 / Table 8 (Skip-gram, 300 dimensions, 783M training words) shows examples, e.g. Paris - France + Italy = Rome.
- Some entries are off in telling ways: for the relationship Japan - sushi the outputs are Germany: bratwurst, France: tapas, USA: pizza. Einstein - scientist gives Mozart: violinist. copper - Cu gives uranium: plutonium. small: larger for big - bigger.
- The authors note that with exact-match scoring, Table 8 would score only about 60%. The famous King - Man + Woman ≈ Queen example is cited in the intro from earlier work (their ref. [20]), not from this table.

## Draft variants

**A, short, table-led:**

In the 2013 word2vec paper, Tomas Mikolov (then at Google) and co-authors show what you get by adding and subtracting word vectors. Paris - France + Italy lands on Rome. Apply Japan - sushi and you get Germany: bratwurst, USA: pizza and France: tapas: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1301.3781#page=10

**B, then vs. now, with a question:**

Embeddings are now the plumbing of most AI search and RAG setups. A lot of that goes back to a 2013 paper by Tomas Mikolov, Kai Chen, Greg Corrado and Jeff Dean at Google, better known as word2vec.

The idea that stuck: train word vectors on lots of text, then do arithmetic on them to answer analogies. The authors are pretty candid about the results in their examples table: "accuracy is quite good, although there is clearly a lot of room for further improvements" (with exact matching, about 60%): https://arxiv.org/pdf/1301.3781#page=10

I'm curious how people here first learned about embeddings. Was it king - man + woman = queen, too? (Apparently that one is from earlier work they cite, not from this paper's table.)

**C, educator's angle, longer:**

A nice detail in the 2013 word2vec paper by Tomas Mikolov (then at Google) and co-authors is how they make "these words are similar" testable.

Instead of showing a list of nearest neighbors and asking readers to nod along, they phrase it as a question: "What is the word that is similar to small in the same sense as biggest is similar to big?" Then they compute biggest - big + small and check which word vector is closest. If the vectors are good, it's "smallest".

The examples table a few pages later is worth a look, partly for the mistakes. Einstein - scientist gives Mozart: violinist. copper - Cu gives uranium: plutonium. Japan - sushi gives France: tapas. The vectors seem to capture "these things show up together" more than the exact relation: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1301.3781#page=10

Feels like a good teaching example, since the wrong answers explain the method almost better than the right ones.
