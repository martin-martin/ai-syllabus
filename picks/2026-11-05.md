# A Proposal for the Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence

**Who:** John McCarthy (Dartmouth College), Marvin Minsky (Harvard), Nathaniel Rochester (IBM) and Claude Shannon (Bell Labs)
**What / when:** research proposal, August 31, 1955 (for a workshop in summer 1956), hosted on McCarthy's Stanford site
**Full piece:** http://jmc.stanford.edu/articles/dartmouth/dartmouth.pdf
**Jump in here:** http://jmc.stanford.edu/articles/dartmouth/dartmouth.pdf#page=2 (page 2: the opening paragraph and the seven "aspects of the artificial intelligence problem"; one page, ~3 min). Second spot for variant C: Rochester's "The Machine With Randomness", #page=8.

## Why this one

This is the document usually credited with coining the term "artificial intelligence", and its first page is a compact list of what four of the field's founders thought the problems were: getting machines to use language, neuron nets, self-improvement, abstraction, and randomness as a source of creativity. Almost every item maps onto something people work on today, which makes it a great then vs. now piece. It's also short, plain-language and free, so anyone can read the first page in a few minutes.

## What's said there

> "We think that a significant advance can be made in one or more of these problems if a carefully selected group of scientists work on it together for a summer." (p. 2, opening paragraph, text verified: yes)

> "The speeds and memory capacities of present computers may be insufficient to simulate many of the higher functions of the human brain, but the major obstacle is not lack of machine capacity, but our inability to write programs taking full advantage of what we have." (p. 2, item 1 "Automatic Computers", text verified: yes)

> "In other words, the educated guess or the hunch include controlled randomness in otherwise orderly thinking." (p. 2, item 7 "Randomness and Creativity", text verified: yes)

> "If, for example, one wrote a program so that once in every 10,000 steps the calculator generated a random number and executed it as an instruction the result would probably be chaos." (p. 8, Rochester, "The Machine With Randomness", text verified: yes)

- The proposal asks for a "2 month, 10 man study" at Dartmouth in summer 1956, based on "the conjecture that every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence can in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be made to simulate it."
- Goals named in the opening: make machines "use language, form abstractions and concepts, solve kinds of problems now reserved for humans, and improve themselves."
- The seven aspects: automatic computers, programming a computer to use a language, neuron nets, theory of the size of a calculation, self-improvement, abstractions, randomness and creativity.
- The estimated budget (p. 4) was $13,500, requested from the Rockefeller Foundation. Each organizer also attached a personal research statement; Minsky's (p. 6) describes a machine that would "build up within itself an abstract model of the environment in which it is placed" and explore solutions there before acting.

## Draft variants

**A, short quote-led:**

In 1955, John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester and Claude Shannon asked the Rockefeller Foundation for $13,500 to study "artificial intelligence" for two months. Their plan: "We think that a significant advance can be made in one or more of these problems if a carefully selected group of scientists work on it together for a summer." http://jmc.stanford.edu/articles/dartmouth/dartmouth.pdf#page=2

**B, then vs. now:**

The 1955 proposal for the Dartmouth workshop (by John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester and Claude Shannon, and apparently where the term "artificial intelligence" comes from) lists seven problems on its first page: http://jmc.stanford.edu/articles/dartmouth/dartmouth.pdf#page=2

Getting a computer to use a language, neuron nets, self-improvement, forming abstractions, randomness and creativity, and two more about computers and computation.

Reading it now, most of these sound like current research topics. Item 1 also says that the speed and memory of computers back then may not be enough, "but the major obstacle is not lack of machine capacity, but our inability to write programs taking full advantage of what we have."

Seventy years later, which of the seven would you count as solved?

**C, educator's angle (randomness):**

One of the seven problems in the 1955 Dartmouth proposal (John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester and Claude Shannon) is "Randomness and Creativity". The idea is that the difference between creative and merely competent thinking might be some injected randomness, guided by intuition: "the educated guess or the hunch include controlled randomness in otherwise orderly thinking."

In his own section, Rochester (IBM) adds that it has to be the right kind of randomness. If a program picked a random instruction once every 10,000 steps, "the result would probably be chaos": http://jmc.stanford.edu/articles/dartmouth/dartmouth.pdf#page=8

He then notes that human errors in speech tend to produce "statements which almost make sense" (when someone is half asleep, or slightly drunk), while computer failures produce something quite unreasonable.

Today's language models sample their next word with an adjustable amount of randomness (the "temperature"), and their mistakes do mostly almost make sense. I wonder whether anyone has traced a line from this 1955 idea to how sampling is taught today?
