# Tracing the thoughts of a large language model

**Who:** Anthropic's interpretability team. The underlying paper "On the Biology of a Large Language Model" lists Jack Lindsey first and Joshua Batson last, with Chris Olah among 27 authors.
**What / when:** blog post, 2025-03-27, anthropic.com (summary of two papers on transformer-circuits.pub)
**Full piece:** https://www.anthropic.com/research/tracing-thoughts-language-model
**Jump in here:** https://www.anthropic.com/research/tracing-thoughts-language-model#mental-math (section "Mental math", 4 paragraphs, ~2 min read)

## Why this one

A current piece on interpretability, i.e. looking inside a model instead of only talking to it. The mental math section is small and concrete (one addition, 36+59) and has a surprising twist: the way the model actually adds differs from how it says it adds. That gap between doing and explaining is a natural angle for an education-minded audience, and it's a break from interviews and videos (text, current era, after the 2017 3Blue1Brown video).

## What's said there

> "Strikingly, Claude seems to be unaware of the sophisticated "mental math" strategies that it learned during training. If you ask how it figured out that 36+59 is 95, it describes the standard algorithm involving carrying the 1." (section "Mental math", text verified: yes)

- Question they start from: how does a model trained only to predict the next word compute 36+59 "in its head" without writing out steps? Two boring hypotheses: it memorized addition tables, or it runs the longhand school algorithm.
- What they found in Claude 3.5 Haiku instead: several paths in parallel. "One path computes a rough approximation of the answer and the other focuses on precisely determining the last digit of the sum." The paths combine into the final answer.
- When asked to explain, the model describes carrying the 1. Their suggested reason: "the model learns to explain math by simulating explanations written by people, but that it has to learn to do math "in its head" directly, without any such hints, and develops its own internal strategies to do so."
- Nearby sections for context: the next one ("Are Claude's explanations always faithful?") shows chain-of-thought sometimes being made up after the fact; the rhyme-planning section ("Does Claude plan its rhymes?") is the other much-cited result, where the team "had set out to show that the model didn't plan ahead, and found instead that it did."

## Draft variants

**A, plain retelling (short):**

Anthropic's interpretability team looked inside Claude 3.5 Haiku while it added 36+59. One internal path estimates the rough size of the answer, another works out the exact last digit. Ask the model how it got 95, though, and it explains carrying the 1: https://www.anthropic.com/research/tracing-thoughts-language-model#mental-math

**B, educator's angle (medium):**

Researchers at Anthropic (Jack Lindsey, Joshua Batson, Chris Olah and colleagues) traced what happens inside a language model when it adds two numbers. It doesn't seem to use the longhand method from school. Instead, one path approximates the answer and another pins down the last digit, and the two get combined.

The part I keep thinking about as someone in education: when asked how it solved 36+59, the model describes the school method anyway. Their guess is that it learned to *explain* math from human-written explanations, but had to figure out how to *do* math on its own: https://www.anthropic.com/research/tracing-thoughts-language-model#mental-math

**C, a question to the network (longer):**

In 2025, Anthropic's interpretability team published a "tour of AI biology", built with a kind of microscope for looking at what happens inside Claude while it answers.

One small example: adding 36+59. They found the model runs a rough estimate and an exact last-digit calculation in parallel and combines them. When asked to explain its work, it describes the standard algorithm with carrying the 1. In their words, Claude "seems to be unaware of the sophisticated "mental math" strategies that it learned during training."

The section right after shows a related problem with longer step-by-step reasoning, where the written-out steps sometimes don't match what happened inside: https://www.anthropic.com/research/tracing-thoughts-language-model#mental-math

Makes me wonder how much we should read into a model's explanation of its own reasoning. (And how often that's true for people explaining how they solved something, too.)
