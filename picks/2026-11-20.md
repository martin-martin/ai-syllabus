# DeepSeek-R1: Incentivizing Reasoning Capability in LLMs via Reinforcement Learning

**Who:** DeepSeek-AI (Daya Guo et al.), DeepSeek (Chinese AI lab, Hangzhou)
**What / when:** paper (arXiv preprint), 22 Jan 2025
**Full piece:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948
**Jump in here:** https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.12948v1#page=8 (section 2.2.4 "Performance, Self-evolution Process and Aha Moment of DeepSeek-R1-Zero" plus Table 3 and "Drawback of DeepSeek-R1-Zero", pages 7 to 9, ~4 min read)

## Why this one

The paper that showed, in the open, that a base model can learn to reason longer (and to double-check itself) from reinforcement learning with a very simple, rule-based reward. It connects straight back to the Atari DQN and Sutton picks: reward, not demonstrations, as the teacher. The "aha moment" section is short, concrete and easy to explain to a broad audience, and the drawbacks paragraph right after it is a nice reality check.

## What's said there

> "Wait, wait. Wait. That's an aha moment I can flag here." (Table 3, p. 9, model output from an intermediate DeepSeek-R1-Zero checkpoint; text verified in the arXiv v1 PDF: yes)

> "rather than explicitly teaching the model on how to solve a problem, we simply provide it with the right incentives, and it autonomously develops advanced problem-solving strategies." (section 2.2.4, p. 8, verified: yes)

- DeepSeek-R1-Zero is trained with RL directly on the base model, no supervised fine-tuning first. The reward is rule-based: is the final answer correct (math answer in a box, code checked against test cases) and is the thinking placed between `<think>` tags (section 2.2.2).
- They deliberately skipped neural reward models because these "may suffer from reward hacking in the large-scale reinforcement learning process" (p. 6).
- Over training, responses get longer on their own, and behaviors like reflection (revisiting earlier steps) "arise spontaneously". Table 3 shows the model stopping mid-solution to re-evaluate, "using an anthropomorphic tone".
- Drawback: R1-Zero "struggles with challenges like poor readability, and language mixing". For the final DeepSeek-R1 they added a language consistency reward, and note that it causes "a slight degradation in the model's performance" but is more readable (p. 10).

**B, educator's question, medium:**

DeepSeek (Chinese AI lab) described in early 2025 how they trained a reasoning model with reinforcement learning and a very simple grader: math answers checked against the solution, code checked against test cases, plus a check that the reasoning sits inside <think> tags. No learned reward model, because they found those can get gamed ("reward hacking").

From that alone, the model apparently started writing longer answers and revisiting its own steps. The researchers' summary: "we simply provide it with the right incentives, and it autonomously develops advanced problem-solving strategies."

Section 2.2.4 is a short read: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.12948v1#page=8

Makes me wonder how far this carries for learning things where there's no answer key to check against. Curious what people think.

**C, the drawbacks paragraph, longer:**

The DeepSeek-R1 paper (DeepSeek-AI, January 2025) is mostly remembered for the "aha moment": a model trained with reinforcement learning, without human-written example solutions first, that started to pause and re-check its own work.

The paragraph right after it is interesting too. That first model, R1-Zero, "struggles with challenges like poor readability, and language mixing", so its reasoning would jump between languages.

For the released DeepSeek-R1 they added a reward for sticking to one language. Their ablations showed this costs "a slight degradation in the model's performance", and they kept it anyway because it's more readable for humans.

So the version that's nicer for us to read is (slightly) worse at the task? https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.12948v1#page=8
