# Zoom In: An Introduction to Circuits

**Who:** Chris Olah, Nick Cammarata, Ludwig Schubert, Gabriel Goh, Michael Petrov, Shan Carter, all at OpenAI at the time (Olah later co-founded Anthropic)
**What / when:** essay (interactive article), March 10, 2020, Distill (part of the "Circuits" thread)
**Full piece:** https://distill.pub/2020/circuits/zoom-in/
**Jump in here:** https://distill.pub/2020/circuits/zoom-in/#claim-2-superposition (section "Circuit 3: Cars in Superposition", 3 short paragraphs plus a diagram, ~2 min)

## Why this one

This is the essay that kicked off the "circuits" style of interpretability: treat single neurons and weights in a vision model as worth studying, the way early microscopists studied cells. The short car/dog section is where the word "superposition" shows up as a named phenomenon, which later became a central idea in Anthropic's interpretability work (including the "tracing thoughts" piece Martin posted on 2026-10-23). It's a good foundational counterpart: same lab lineage, five years earlier, on an image model instead of an LLM.

## What's said there

> "But what we’re seeing here is that the model had a “pure neuron” and then mixed it up with other features. We call this phenomenon superposition." (section "Circuit 3: Cars in Superposition", text verified: yes, fetched from distill.pub)

> "As long as cars and dogs don’t co-occur, the model can accurately retrieve the dog feature in a later layer, allowing it to store the feature without dedicating a neuron." (same section, text verified: yes)

- In a mid-late layer of InceptionV1 (mixed4c), there's a clean car-detecting neuron, built from wheel detectors (bottom of its window) and window detectors (top).
- In the next layer, instead of another pure car neuron, the model spreads the car feature over several neurons that mostly detect dogs.
- The authors read this as deliberate: the model had a "pure neuron" and chose to mix it. Their guess is that it saves neurons for more important things.
- The explanation is geometric: a high-dimensional space has only n orthogonal directions, but exponentially many almost-orthogonal ones.
- Context elsewhere in the essay: the intro compares the field to Hooke's Micrographia and the early microscope, and a later section ("Interpretability as a Natural Science", #natural-science) argues for treating networks "perhaps similar to an organism in biology". Could be an alternative angle given Martin's biology background.

**C, longer, educator's angle with a question:**

Chris Olah (interpretability researcher, later co-founder of Anthropic) and colleagues at OpenAI wrote "Zoom In" in 2020. The idea: instead of explaining a whole neural network at once, look at single neurons and their connections, like a biologist with a microscope.

One example: in an image model, there's a neuron that detects cars, put together from wheel detectors at the bottom and window detectors at the top. In the next layer, the model doesn't keep a clean car neuron. It spreads "car" across several neurons that mostly detect dogs.

Why would it do that? Their explanation is that this saves neurons, and it works "as long as cars and dogs don’t co-occur". The math behind it: in a space with n dimensions you only get n directions at right angles to each other, but exponentially many that are almost at right angles.

They called this superposition, and it later became one of the central ideas in interpretability research: https://distill.pub/2020/circuits/zoom-in/#claim-2-superposition

I wonder how much of this carries over to how people store concepts. Anyone from neuroscience who knows if there's a comparable idea there?
