# Accelerating scientific discovery with AI (Nobel Lecture)

**Who:** Demis Hassabis, Google DeepMind (co-founder and CEO)
**What / when:** lecture (Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2024, shared with John Jumper and David Baker), 8 December 2024, Aula Magna, Stockholm University
**Full piece:** https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/2024/hassabis/lecture/ (video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YtPaZsasmNA)
**Jump in here:** https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2024/12/hassabis-lecture.pdf#page=19 (slides 19 to 23, "Making search tractable" through "What are the limits of classical systems?", ~3 min read)

## Why this one

Hassabis's lecture is the bigger-picture companion to John Jumper's (picked 2026-11-06): instead of how AlphaFold works, he steps back and asks what AlphaGo and AlphaFold have in common. His answer is a general recipe (learn a model, use it to guide search), and then a bold conjecture about what classical learning algorithms can model in nature. It's a clear example of a researcher generalizing from two systems to a claim about science and even complexity theory, which makes for a good discussion piece.

## What's said there

> "Any pattern that can be generated or found in nature can be efficiently discovered and modelled by a classical learning algorithm" (slide 23, labeled "My Proposed Conjecture", verified in the lecture slides PDF: yes)

> "Finding the optimal solution in an enormous combinatorial space" (slide 19, his answer to "what is the essence of what our systems are doing?", verified: yes)

- Slide 7 sets up what makes a problem suitable for AI: a "Massive combinatorial search space", a "Clear objective function (metric) to optimise against", and "Either lots of data and/or an accurate and efficient simulator".
- Slide 19: the common recipe behind AlphaGo and AlphaFold is to "Learn a model of that environment (from data or simulation)" and "Use that model to guide a search according to an objective function". "Turns out this is a very general solution and many problems fit this approach."
- Slides 20 and 21 put the Go move search and the search for molecules in chemical space side by side.
- Slide 23: "Classical Turing Machines can do much more than we previously thought", by doing "a massive amount of pre-compute upfront to develop a good model", then the conjecture. If classical systems can model certain quantum systems, "it could potentially have big implications for complexity theory including P=NP, and maybe even fundamental physics!"
