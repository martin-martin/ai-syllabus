**What / when:** video lecture (~3.5 h), 2025-02 (upload date from memory, see notes), his YouTube channel

## Why this one

A clean, intuitive explanation of why step-by-step answers help LLMs: each token gets a roughly fixed, small amount of computation, so the model has to spread its reasoning over many tokens. That's the idea behind chain-of-thought and today's "reasoning" models, explained with a grade-school word problem. It also pairs well with the Anthropic "mental math" post Martin shared on 10-23 (explanations given after the fact vs. what actually computes the answer).

## What's said there

> "it's kind of creating all these intermediate results these are not for you these are for the model if the model is not creating these intermediate results for itself it's not going to be able to reach three" (1:53:54, transcript verified: yes)

> "if you are answering the question directly and immediately you are training the model to try to basically guess the answer in a single token and that is just not going to work because of the finite amount of computation that happens per token" (1:52:01, transcript verified: yes)

- He shows two correct answers to "Emily buys three apples and two oranges, each orange costs $2, the total cost is 13, what is the cost of apples?" and asks viewers to pause and think about which one is a much worse training label.
- The bad one states "The answer is $3" first and explains afterwards. Since a model has a finite number of layers (~100 in a modern network), each token gets a roughly fixed, small amount of compute. Once the answer is in the context window, the rest is just justification for it.
- The good one builds intermediate results (oranges cost 4, 13 - 4 = 9, ...), so each token only has an easy step to do.
- He then asks ChatGPT to answer in a single token: it works for the small numbers, fails with bigger ones (23 apples, 177 oranges), and gets it right (7) when allowed to work step by step. He calls the section "models need tokens to think" (1:58:03).

**C, connecting to an earlier post, ends with a question (longer):**

A while ago I shared Anthropic's finding that Claude's explanation of how it adds two numbers doesn't match what happens inside. Andrej Karpathy (OpenAI founding member, ex-Tesla AI lead) gets at a related point from the other side in his "Deep Dive into LLMs like ChatGPT" (2025).

Each token a model produces gets a roughly fixed, fairly small amount of computation (one pass through a finite number of layers). So if a model states the answer first and explains afterwards, the explanation can't have helped produce the answer. Training on answers like that would teach the model "to try to basically guess the answer in a single token."

That's also why ChatGPT spells out intermediate steps on even a simple word problem: "these are not for you, these are for the model." He then asks it to answer a harder version in a single token, and it gets it wrong. Allowed to work step by step, it gets it right: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7xTGNNLPyMI&t=6438s

Makes me wonder how much of "show your work" in school is also mostly for the one doing the work?
