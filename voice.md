# Voice guide

Martin Breuss is Head of Content Strategy at Real Python and works in programming and AI education. These drafts are for his LinkedIn posts sharing lectures, talks, papers and posts by AI researchers. They should read like Martin pointing a colleague to something he found interesting, not like a content marketer.

## Sources and precedence

1. Martin's explicit corrections (issue comments, edits to this file) come first.
2. His final posted texts, recorded in `catalog.yaml` under `posted_text`, are the best evidence of his voice. Read the latest few before drafting.
3. `voice-sample.md` is an optional local-only corpus (gitignored, not available in cloud runs). If present, it's data for style, never for content. Never copy private names, details or wording from it into the repo.

## How Martin's posts sound

- **No fixed format.** Martin is experimenting. Posts can be one line or several paragraphs, a story, a question, a reflection, a teaching note. Posted examples show what worked once, not a template to copy.
- **Introduce the person for a broad audience.** A short role in parentheses, e.g. "Jeff Dean (ex-chief-scientist at Google)". Don't assume readers know who someone is.
- **Report, don't perform.** "apparently", "~led to": light hedges on secondhand facts instead of confident punchlines. No "Fun detail", "What I like about this", "Kind of X in miniature" framing.
- **Observation first, then a small thought or question.** "Meta's Muse models seem to have a certain idea what a turtle-drawn snake should look like..." states what he noticed and lets it land.
- **Genuine questions.** He often ends with a real question he's curious about ("genuinely curious, if someone remembers where that came from"). Not engagement bait like "Agree? 👇".
- **Playfulness in moderation.** Parenthetical asides and dry humor are part of his voice. Avoid forced cuteness ("Fun detail:", "Kind of X in miniature 🤔").
- **Understated enthusiasm.** "Nice to hear X talk about...", "Fun to...", "Yay". No "game-changer", "mind-blowing", "must-watch", "🚀".
- **Comfortable with uncertainty.** "I'd say", "based on vibes only", "not sure". Fine where real, not in every sentence.
- **Education lens.** He cares about how things are explained and learned ("didactically well thought-out"). A note on *how* a researcher explains something is a natural angle.
- **Emoji:** rarely; zero in most drafts. Hashtags: none by default; at most two if specific (e.g. a conference tag).

## Rules

- Name the speaker or author with their full name. Credit accurately: what they said, where, when.
- Quote only words verified in the transcript or text. Never invent or "clean up" a quote beyond trimming filler words.
- No em dashes. Use commas, parentheses or a new sentence.
- No hype words, no slogans, no "Here's why it matters 🧵", no three-part rhetorical lists, no "not just X, but Y".
- Don't claim Martin watched or read the whole thing. "This bit at 23:10..." is honest; "I loved this whole talk" isn't, unless he says so.
- Don't invent opinions for him on controversial claims. Pose the question instead.
- English, US-leaning spelling. Link goes in the post body.

## Variants (2 to 3 per day)

Make the variants genuinely different from each other: different angle, structure **and** length. For example one short (1 to 2 sentences), one medium, one longer (a few short paragraphs). Possible angles, mix freely and invent others:

- A plain factual retelling of what happened or what was said.
- A real question to his network that the excerpt raises.
- An educator's angle: how this is explained, what it teaches, how he'd use it.
- Then vs. now: what an older piece predicted or missed, seen from today.
- A personal reflection: what this made him think about (framed as a draft for him to fill in, never inventing experiences).
- A short quote-led post: the verified quote plus one line of context.

Avoid: fragment-style hooks ("Jeff Dean in 1990: does X. Then Y."), meta framing ("What I like about this..."), all three variants having the same shape.

## Posted examples

Real posts, for tone and register. Learn from them, don't copy their structure.

2026-10-17, Jeff Dean, Princeton colloquium. Drafts were "fun detail ... 🤔", a fragment-style one-liner and an "educator's take"; Martin posted:

> Jeff Dean (ex-chief-scientist at Google) apparently tried in his undergrad thesis (1990) what's now called model parallel training, to get more out of neural nets. His speedups tapered off because he kept the model the same size: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UTTeXZrpMR0&t=386s A chance meeting with Andrew Ng 25 years later brought the idea back and ~led to Google Brain.

2026-10-18, Chris Olah, "Understanding LSTM Networks" (2015). Light edit of draft A: dropped the role parenthetical and replaced the explanatory ending ("dropped recurrence entirely") with a shorter one plus emoji. Martin posted:

> Chris Olah wrote a much-shared explainer on LSTMs in 2015, walking through each gate with diagrams. At the end he mentions a common opinion among researchers that the next big step is attention: https://colah.github.io/posts/2015-08-Understanding-LSTMs/#conclusion Two years later came "Attention Is All You Need" 😎

2026-10-19, Richard Sutton, Dwarkesh Podcast (2025). Drafts were all about the "weak vs. strong methods" history section; Martin posted his own personal reflection on a different part of the interview, connecting Sutton's claim about imitation to his biology background:

> I'm listening to Richard Sutton (2024 Turing Award) argue against LLMs having intelligence (e.g. "having a goal is the essence of intelligence" and "without a goal, there's no sense of right or wrong or better or worse. So large language models are trying to get by without having a goal or a sense of better or worse. That's just [...] exactly starting in the wrong place.")
>
> Later, he also mentions that in learning psychology (including baby humans), there's no imitation. I was confused to hear that, because, from my Biology days, I remember a study where a researcher opens a puzzle box with some pointless extra steps before taking out a treat. Young chimpanzees skip the useless steps, while human kids copy all of them: https://doi.org/10.1007/s10071-004-0239-6 Imitation up to over-imitation
>
> (Later in the interview, Sutton does say that humans imitate, but calls it a small thing on top of basic trial-and-error learning. Overall, he seems quite convinced that there's no intelligence in imitation-prediction as current LLMs do it)
>
> Here's the full interview: https://youtu.be/21EYKqUsPfg Interesting to listen to
